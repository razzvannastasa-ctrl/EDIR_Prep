HANDBOOK OF FOOTBALL ASSOCIATION MANAGEMENT



The Europa League is UEFA’s second club competition (UEFA.com, n.d. (f)). Originally launched in 1971, in
2009 it was reorganised and rebranded with its current name. As of the current club competition cycle, the
winners automatically qualify for the group stage of the Champions League the following season.

The bulk of the revenue generated by the Champions League and the Europa League is distributed to
participating clubs, with €1.97bn and €0.56bn being distributed to Champions League and Europa League
clubs respectively in 2018/19. However, UEFA also distributes revenue to clubs that have not taken part in
the two competitions in the form of solidarity payments, with such payments totalling €128.69m in 2018/19.
That revenue must be invested in football development projects, with particular emphasis being placed on
youth development. The solidarity payments are a physical manifestation of UEFA’s adherence to the principle
of sporting solidarity (redistribution of resources from the elite professional game to the grassroots) set out
in its statutes (UEFA.com, 30 December 2019).

For the 2021–24 club competition cycle, UEFA is launching a third club competition, the Europa Conference
League, which starts with a 32-team group stage, before switching to a knockout format (UEFA.com,
31 October 2019). The winners of this new competition will automatically qualify for the Europa League the
following season. Critically, this will ensure that at least 34 countries are represented in the group stages of
UEFA’s various club competitions, up from a minimum of 26 in the current cycle. When the Europa Conference
League is launched in 2021/22, the number of clubs competing in the group stage of the Europa League
will be reduced from 48 to 32.

For the 2013/14 season, UEFA launched a major new youth competition, the Youth League. That competition
is contested by the Under-19 teams of the clubs participating in the group stage of the UEFA Champions
League and the Under-19 league champions of UEFA’s member associations (UEFA.com, n.d. (g)).

The European Women’s Championship is UEFA’s competition for senior women’s national teams. The first
edition was contested between 1982 and 1984, with Sweden defeating England in the final (UEFA.com,
n.d. (h)). The competition has since evolved into one of the biggest events in women’s sport. The final round
of the 2015–17 edition was held in the Netherlands and won by the hosts, with the final being watched
by a global television audience of 15 million, while 178 million people watched the final tournament as a
whole (UEFA, 2017a, p. 88). The final round of the next European Women’s Championship will take place
in England in 2022.

The Women’s Champions League is the biggest international women’s club competition in the world
(UEFA.com, n.d. (i)). The competition was launched in 2001/02 as the Women’s Cup, before being rebranded
as the Women’s Champions League in 2009/10. A record 62 teams from 50 countries entered the 2019/20
competition, which was won by Olympic Lyonnais of France. The success of the competition can be seen
in the evolution of its structure, which will include a new 16-team group stage as of 2021/22 (UEFA.com,
6 October 2020).
